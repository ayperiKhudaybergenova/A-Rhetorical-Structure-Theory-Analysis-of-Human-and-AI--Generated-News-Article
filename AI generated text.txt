Even camels can't cope: Africa's ships of the desert hit by rising temperatures

For centuries, the camel has been the ultimate survivor of Africa's drylands — an animal so well adapted to heat and thirst that it became a symbol of endurance itself. Herders across the Horn of Africa have long said that where cattle and goats perish, the camel walks on. But that assumption is now being tested. Across Ethiopia, Somalia, Kenya and parts of North Africa, veterinarians, pastoralists and researchers are reporting something that would once have seemed unthinkable: camels are struggling to cope with the heat.
The animal renowned as the "ship of the desert" evolved to withstand extreme temperatures, water scarcity and long migrations across the planet's harshest terrain. Yet prolonged drought, rising average temperatures and increasingly erratic rainfall are pushing camel herds — and the millions of people who depend on them — closer to a breaking point than at any point in living memory.
## A herder's changing world
Hassan Abdi Nur has kept camels in Kenya's Marsabit County for most of his life, following a route his father and grandfather once walked. In previous decades, he says, the family could rely on established wet and dry seasons to guide their movements between pasture and water. That predictability has disappeared.
"My father used to know exactly when the rains would come and where the grass would be green," Nur said. "Now we wait, and wait, and sometimes nothing comes at all. Even the camels, which can go many days without water, are collapsing on the road when we move them too far in this heat."
Nur said he lost four camels during the drought that struck the region between 2020 and 2023, one of the worst in the Horn of Africa in four decades. Two died of dehydration after a longer trek to a water point that had itself begun to dry up. Another two succumbed to illness he believes was worsened by heat stress and poor grazing.
"We always said the camel does not die of thirst like other animals," he said. "But I have seen it happen with my own eyes now."
Herders in neighbouring Somalia and Ethiopia's Somali region describe similar experiences. In pastoralist communities where camels represent not just a source of milk and transport but a measure of family wealth and status, the animal's declining resilience is felt as both an economic and a cultural loss.
## What the evidence shows
Herders' testimony is increasingly backed by scientific data. Meteorological records for the Horn of Africa show average temperatures have risen markedly since the 1980s, with some national weather services recording increases of close to 1.5°C over four decades — faster than the global average in several areas. Rainfall has become both scarcer and less predictable, with the region experiencing a series of failed or below-average rainy seasons in the early 2020s.
Regional climate monitoring bodies, including IGAD's Climate Prediction and Applications Centre, have documented at least five consecutive failed rainy seasons across parts of Kenya, Somalia and Ethiopia between late 2020 and early 2023 — a sequence without clear precedent in the region's historical record. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization has estimated that millions of livestock, including camels, died as a result, with pastoralist communities losing a significant share of their herds.
"We used to think of camels as almost drought-proof animals," said Dr Amina Yusuf, a veterinary researcher who has studied livestock health in arid areas of East Africa. "What we are now seeing is that even a species this well adapted has physiological limits, and those limits are being reached more often than before."
Camel populations in parts of the Horn of Africa have continued to grow in absolute numbers, partly because herders have shifted away from more heat-vulnerable cattle. But researchers caution that rising ownership does not necessarily mean rising resilience: herd productivity — milk yield, calving rates and body condition — is, by several regional accounts, declining even where overall numbers remain stable.
## Why heat is proving so damaging
Camels are, by any measure, extraordinarily well suited to hot, dry environments. They can tolerate body temperature fluctuations of several degrees over the day, avoiding excessive sweating. Their humps store fat rather than water, providing an energy reserve without the insulating effect of fat spread across the body. They can go without drinking for a week or more, then consume enormous quantities of water in a single sitting to rehydrate.
These adaptations, however, are calibrated to a particular range of conditions — conditions that scientists say are shifting.
"The issue is not simply that it is hot — deserts have always been hot," explained Professor Bernard Kiptoo, an animal physiologist who studies heat tolerance in livestock. "The issue is the combination of higher baseline temperatures, longer heatwaves, and shorter recovery periods between droughts. A camel that could once rest and recover its body condition between dry spells no longer has that opportunity."
Reduced, less predictable rainfall also affects the quality and availability of forage. Camels are browsers, feeding on shrubs and trees rather than grass, and prolonged drought reduces both the quantity and nutritional value of vegetation available. Weakened animals become more vulnerable to disease, less able to walk the long distances now required to reach water and pasture, and less able to regulate body temperature even within their normal range.
## A problem across the region
While the Horn of Africa has drawn particular attention because of the severity of its recent droughts, the pressures on camel herding are visible across a wide swathe of the continent.
In Somalia, where camels are central to the rural economy and national identity, officials and aid agencies reported substantial livestock losses during the 2020–2023 drought, with camels among the animals affected despite their reputation for resilience. In Ethiopia's lowland pastoralist regions, including Afar and the Somali region, herders describe longer migration routes as grazing areas become depleted, increasing physical strain on animals already coping with heat stress. Kenya's arid counties, home to a large share of the country's camel population, have seen similar patterns, with Marsabit, Wajir and Garissa among the areas most affected.
Further north, in parts of the Sahara and Sahel, herders and researchers describe a slower but comparable trend: rising temperatures, shifting rainfall, and growing competition for shrinking water resources, sometimes worsened by conflict and restricted mobility across borders.
"What we are seeing is not confined to one country or one drought cycle," said Dr Yusuf. "It is a broader pattern across the continent's drylands, and camel herding communities in different regions are independently describing very similar changes."
## The biological toll
Veterinarians and researchers point to several interlinked biological effects when camels are exposed to prolonged heat stress and water scarcity.
Dehydration is the most immediate concern. Although camels tolerate significant fluid loss compared with other livestock, extended periods without adequate water still impair kidney function and circulation, and in severe cases can be fatal, particularly for young, old or already weakened animals.
Heat stress also appears to suppress immune function, making camels more susceptible to disease. Veterinarians in affected regions report increased respiratory illness and parasitic infections during and after periods of extreme heat and drought, when animals are already in poorer body condition.
Milk production, a critical resource for pastoralist households, is particularly sensitive to heat and nutritional stress. "Milk yield is one of the first things to drop when a camel is under heat stress and short of good forage," said Dr Yusuf. "For families that depend on that milk for nutrition and income, even a modest decline can have a significant impact."
Reproductive performance is also affected. Camels already have long gestation periods and low reproduction rates, calving perhaps once every two years under good conditions. Researchers say heat and nutritional stress can further delay conception, reduce calving rates, and increase calf mortality, slowing herd recovery even once conditions improve.
Taken together, these effects — dehydration, disease, reduced milk yield, reproductive setbacks and, in the most severe cases, death — represent a significant threat to an animal whose primary value to pastoralist communities lies in precisely those functions: providing food, transport and a store of wealth that can be drawn upon in hard times.
## Consequences beyond the herd
The implications extend well beyond individual animals. Camels underpin the economies of pastoralist communities across large parts of the Horn of Africa and the Sahara, supporting an estimated tens of millions of people directly or indirectly, according to regional livestock body estimates. When camel productivity declines, the effects ripple outward: reduced milk for household consumption and sale, weakened animals fetching lower market prices, and diminished capacity to transport goods and water.
There are also social and cultural dimensions. In many pastoralist societies, camel ownership is tied to status, marriage arrangements and community identity. The loss of animals during drought can force families into difficult decisions, including selling remaining livestock at depressed prices or moving to urban areas — a shift aid organisations say is already under way in parts of the region.
"When people lose their camels, they are not just losing an economic asset," said Fatuma Ali Warsame, a community development worker who has worked with pastoralist groups in Ethiopia's Somali region. "They are losing a way of life that has structured their communities for generations."
## Looking ahead
Climate projections for the Horn of Africa generally point towards continued warming and greater rainfall variability in the coming decades, according to assessments referenced by regional climate bodies, though the scale and pace of change remain uncertain. If current trends continue, researchers warn that camel herding — long regarded as a climate-resilient adaptation strategy in its own right — will face growing pressure.
Efforts to respond are already under way. Veterinary services in some areas have expanded mobile clinics to reach herds during droughts, while researchers study selective breeding for heat and drought tolerance. Early warning systems tracking rainfall and pasture conditions are helping some herders plan migration routes before conditions deteriorate, and some development programmes support diversifying pastoralist livelihoods.
"There is no single solution," said Professor Kiptoo. "It will require better veterinary support, smarter use of climate information, investment in water infrastructure, and, ultimately, broader efforts to slow the pace of warming itself. Camels have adapted to change before, over thousands of years, but the pace of change now is faster than anything in their evolutionary history."
Some researchers also point to the need for policies supporting herder mobility, noting that restricted access to traditional grazing and water points — due to land use changes, border restrictions or conflict — can compound the physiological stress camels already face from heat and drought.
## A resilience under strain
For Hassan Abdi Nur, the technical explanations matter less than what he sees on the ground. He has begun keeping fewer camels than his father did, hoping a smaller herd will be easier to sustain through frequent dry spells.
"We are not giving up on the camel," he said. "It is still the best animal for this land. But we are learning that we cannot depend on it the way we used to, without also thinking about the water, the grass, and the weather changing around us."
His words capture the wider story now unfolding across Africa's drylands. The camel remains one of the best-adapted large mammals on Earth for life in extreme heat and aridity, an animal that has allowed human communities to thrive in some of the planet's harshest environments for thousands of years. But as rising temperatures, prolonged droughts and erratic rainfall test the outer limits of that adaptation, even the ship of the desert is showing signs of strain. What was once considered nearly indestructible is proving, after all, to have its limits — and the communities built around it are having to adapt in turn.
